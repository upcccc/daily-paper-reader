Abstract 

Regulatory audits of AI systems increasingly rely on differential privacy (DP) to protect training data and model internals. We study audit design when the au-dited developer can strategically respond to the privacy-constrained audit interface. We formalize privacy-constrained auditing as a bilevel Stackelberg game, in which an auditor commits to a query policy and DP budget allocation across harm di-mensions, and a strategic developer reallocates mitigation efforts in response. We introduce the welfare-weighted under-detection gap 𝐵 𝑤 , the welfare-weighted true residual harm the audit fails to detect at the developer’s strategic best response, and prove that naive DP auditing (uniform or harm-proportional allocation) in-duces a strictly larger 𝐵 𝑤 than any non-strategic mitigation baseline whenever effective detectability is heterogeneous, the welfare weights are not comonotone with detectability, and the developer’s optimum is interior. We characterize the optimal auditor allocation as a four-factor balance of welfare weight, audit miss-probability, detectability elasticity, and mitigation-cost curvature, and provide a single-level reformulation of the bilevel problem via the developer’s KKT system. We propose Strategic Private Audit Design (SPAD), a projected-gradient algorithm with hypergradients computed through the developer’s best response. We specify a synthetic evaluation protocol across harm dimensions, developer behaviors, and pri-vacy budgets under quadratic mitigation costs and exponential residual-harm reduc-tion; preliminary experiments show that SPAD reduces the welfare-weighted under-detection gap 𝐵 𝑤 by 5–14% under uniform welfare and 20–44% under non-uniform welfare across the tested privacy-budget regimes, beating welfare-proportional allo-cation by 13–20% in the non-uniform-welfare case. 

Keywords: differential privacy, auditing, strategic response, Stackelberg games, mechanism design

# 1 Introduction 

Differential privacy is standard for protecting individuals during audits of machine learn-ing systems. From fairness audits assessing algorithmic bias to safety evaluations probing model robustness, privacy-preserving mechanisms ensure auditors cannot infer sensitive information about data points or models. However, this protection has a subtle cost. Allocating privacy budgets across harm dimensions creates uneven observability : some dimensions become harder to detect due to variations in query frequency, privacy budget, or detection sensitivity. This asymmetry protects privacy but creates strategic incentives for the audited system. Consider a strategic developer operating the audited system, facing queries about performance failures across dimensions, like false positive rates on subgroups or safety lapses in corner cases. We use developer for this strategic agent, whether an ML platform, model provider, or in-house team. If the developer anticipates some dimensions will be noisier, less frequently queried, or assigned smaller privacy budgets, it has an incentive to reallocate mitigation efforts away from low-detectability regions and concentrate harms there. The privacy mechanism, meant to protect privacy during audits, creates an oppor-tunity for the developer to hide failures in less observable areas. This tension between privacy as protection and audit evasion defines the core problem we address. We ask: if an auditor knows the developer will behave strategically in response to the audit interface, how should the auditor design queries and privacy budgets to minimize true undetected harm? Strategic responses to evaluations have been studied in the context of classifier games and strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2018), where agents learn to manipulate their features when they know they will be evaluated. Audit games have been modeled as bilevel competitions between an auditor who chooses what to inspect and an adversary who reallocates defensive resources in response (Blocki et al., 2015). Privacy auditing has simultaneously grown into a mature empirical field, with demon-strated attacks on trained models showing that information leakage is detectable and 1closely tracked by theoretical differential privacy (DP) bounds (Tramèr et al., 2022; Nasr, Hayes, et al., 2023). However, no prior work jointly models the critical combination of three structural elements: (a) DP composition and the auditor’s allocation of privacy budgets across multiple harm dimensions; (b) the auditor’s commitment to a fixed query policy and privacy allocation, which the strategic developer can observe before choosing its own actions; and (c) the strategic developer’s best-response reallocation of mitigation effort, subject to a cost budget and aiming to minimize audit exposure. The gap is struc-tural. Privacy-auditing papers design audits without modeling the strategic response to the audit interface itself. Strategic-classification papers model agent responses but as-sume fully observed features, not privacy-noised observations. Audit-game papers do not incorporate DP constraints at all and thus do not capture how detectability heterogene-ity created by privacy noise changes incentives. The triple intersection—DP-constrained observation, auditor commitment, and strategic developer response—remains unexplored and is the setting of this study. We formalize this gap as a Stackelberg game between an auditor and a developer. Astrategically rational developer, anticipating this commitment and knowing which dimen-sions are noisier and less queried, solves its own optimization problem: it reallocates mitigation efforts to minimize expected audit exposure (weighted by detectability) plus mitigation cost, thereby concentrating residual harm in dimensions where the effective detectability 𝜋 𝑗 𝛼 𝑗 (𝜀 𝑗 ) is lowest. # 4 Optimal Private Audit Design 

We now characterize the optimal audit design, which is the allocation that minimizes the welfare-weighted under-detection gap 𝐵 𝑤 when the developer responds rationally. We 10 show three results: (i) the optimal design is fundamentally non-proportional to baseline harm (Theorem 4.1); (ii) the bilevel problem admits a tractable single-level reformulation via the developer’s KKT conditions (Theorem 4.2); and (iii) we provide an algorithm, Strategic Private Audit Design (SPAD).

# 6 Related Work 

Privacy and audit-policy literatures. DP-specific audits (Fredrikson et al., 2015; Shokri, 2015; Nasr, Shokri, et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2022; Carlini et al., 2023; Tramèr et al., 2022; Nasr, Hayes, et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2019; Naseri et al., 2022; Ponomareva et al., 2023) test whether implementations satisfy DP guarantees ; we instead use DP as an observability constraint in audits of downstream harms against a developer that reallocates defenses. Yang et al. (2023) adopt a Stackelberg DP-auditing framing focused on herd auditing (not per-dimension budget allocation); Das et al. (2026) study principal–agent audit-policy design with adversarial equilibrium selection but without DP composition. 

Strategic agents, audit games, and privacy mechanisms. Strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018) and performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020) model agent gaming but not multidimensional reallocation under noised audits; audit games (Blocki et al., 2015) and security games (Korzhyk et al., 2011; Schlenker et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2019) supply the bilevel template but assume clean observations. Privacy-aware mechanism design and adaptive composition (Nissim et al., 2012; Ghosh and Ligett, 2013; Duchi et al., 2013; Bun et al., 2018; Bassily et al., 2016; Bu et al., 2020; Dwork et al., 2019) treat 𝜀 as a budget, not a per-dimension Stackelberg choice. MPC-based fairness monitoring (He et al., 2026) targets post-market fairness audits in algorithmic hiring under MPC threat models, and is complementary to our DP-noised strategic-developer regime. No prior work jointly addresses DP composition, per-dimension commitment, and strategic mitigation reallocation. Limitations: one-shot commitment, exact best-response, mitigation-only adaptation; relaxations (repeated au-dits, noisy 𝜀 , concealment, bounded rationality) preserve Theorem 3.2 but not SPAD 16 optimality.

This work is motivated by the growing deployment of differential privacy in AI governance contexts, including fairness audits under data-protection regulation and safety evaluations of high-risk AI systems. The primary positive impact is analytical: by demonstrating that a naïve DP audit design can systematically miss harm concentrated in low-detectability dimensions, the paper provides a principled basis for regulatory bodies to demand strate-gic audit design. SPAD could be adopted by independent audit firms and compliance teams as a practical tool for allocating limited audit budgets across regulatory harm dimensions (e.g., demographic subgroups, failure modes, and protected attributes). We identify two potential negative societal impacts that warrant discussion. First, adversarial use by auditors. An auditor with strategic interests—for example, an indus-try self-regulator with commercial ties to the developer—could misuse SPAD to design audits that minimize regulatory exposure rather than true harm. The framework’s as-sumption that the auditor’s welfare weights 𝑤 reflect genuine social priorities is critical; if 𝑤 is manipulated, the SPAD optimum can be gamed by the auditor rather than by the developer. Safeguards such as independent certification of 𝑤 and public disclosure of audit-design parameters are warranted in practice. 

Second, strategic complexity as a barrier to adoption. The SPAD algorithm requires knowledge of the developer’s cost structure (𝑐 𝑗 , 𝑔 𝑗 ) and mitigation budget 𝐵 to solve the bilevel problem. Regulators with limited technical capacity may find the framework inaccessible relative to simpler heuristic policies. We recommend that future work de-velop simplified audit-design rules, for example, threshold-based budget allocation, that preserve most of the blind-spot reduction achievable by SPAD without requiring full developer-type knowledge.
